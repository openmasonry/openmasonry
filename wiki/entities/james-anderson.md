# James Anderson (c. 1679–1739)

Scottish Presbyterian minister settled in London. He is the principal author of the **Constitutions of 1723** (*The Constitutions of the Free-Masons*), the founding text of modern speculative Freemasonry.

## What he did
- **1723:** at the Grand Lodge's request, he "digested" the old stonemasons' manuscripts ([[old-charges]]) into a book with a legendary history, **six Charges** and George Payne's **39 General Regulations**. The dedication is by [[jean-theophile-desaguliers]].
- **1738:** he published the second edition, with a greatly expanded history. It contains, **for the first time**, the account of the founding of the Grand Lodge on Jun 24, 1717.

## What is disputed
- **Authorship:** some historians attribute the decisive content to Desaguliers (the Charges) and to Payne (the Regulations), and see Anderson as an editor.
- **Reliability:** his history going back to Adam is legend. The 1717 account, written twenty years later, is the only source for the event, and Prescott and Sommers (2017) propose 1721 as the documented beginning. See [[hist-anderson-constitutions-1717]].

## Legacy
- **Charge I** detached Freemasonry from any specific religious confession ("that Religion in which all Men agree"). It is the basis of the debate over the [[great-architect-of-the-universe]].
- **Charge III** excluded women and "bondmen", setting down in writing the [[regularity-and-recognition|exclusion]] that mixed grand lodges would later contest.
- **Regulation XXXIX** created the idea of unchangeable [[landmarks]] without ever defining them, while also requiring a majority of those present, "even of the youngest Apprentice", to change the rules.

## For OpenMasonry
OpenMasonry inherits from Anderson the idea of a **written constitution** and of rules amendable by majority. It replaces the undefined landmarks with seven **explicit, code-verifiable** Landmarks (whitepaper §3).

# Enlightenment Sociability

The historiographical concept that treats the clubs, academies, coffee houses, salons and Masonic lodges of the 18th century as **spaces of sociability**: places where people of different conditions met as "equals" to debate, vote and govern themselves. It was popularized by the French historian Maurice Agulhon and is in dialogue with Habermas's "public sphere".

**The thesis**
- **Margaret C. Jacob** (*Living the Enlightenment*, 1991): lodges were among the main enclaves in which modern civil society was formed. They were self-government in miniature, with constitutions, laws, elections and representatives, and in them philosophers, merchants and officials practiced constitutional life before their states did.

**Readings and caveats**
- **Optimistic:** lodges were schools of citizenship and tolerance.
- **Critical:** equality was internal and limited to men of a certain standing. The same network that formed citizens could form factions, and the extreme case is [[p2-lodge]].

## In OpenMasonry
- The whitepaper takes this historiography as its starting point (§1.1): the "lodge" form solved real problems, such as adult learning, trust between strangers and constitutional practice.
- The project's central thesis is the dark side of the same sociability: "Power accumulates where three things meet: secrecy, discretion and permanence" (§1.3). OpenMasonry keeps the school of government and removes all three: rules, records, decisions and teaching are public (Landmark II), every rule is tested code (VI) and no power is permanent (V). See [[eternal-landmarks-of-openmasonry]].
- Constitutional practice stops being a simulation: members vote on real, hash-pinned amendments to the rules that govern them (see [[decision-function]], [[ostrom-principles]]).

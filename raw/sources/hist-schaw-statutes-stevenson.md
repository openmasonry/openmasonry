## William Schaw
- c. 1550–1602. Appointed Master of Works to King James VI in 1583 (authority over royal buildings); styled "General Warden" of the masons of Scotland.

## First Statutes: December 28, 1598, Edinburgh (paraphrase of the provisions)
- Masons must obey their wardens, deacons and masters; each lodge elects a warden **every year**.
- A master may take no more than three apprentices in his lifetime without special consent; apprenticeship lasts at least seven years; each apprentice's name and date of entry must be **booked** (recorded).
- A new fellow of craft or master is admitted only with six masters and two entered apprentices present, one of them the warden; his name and **mark** are written in the book.
- No work alongside "cowans" (unqualified/unlicensed builders); fines (e.g. £20 per offense; £10 for not attending a summoned meeting).
- Modern transcription line: "That no master or fellow of craft receive any cowans to work in his presence or company."

## Second Statutes: December 28, 1599, Holyrood House
- Revised after lodges objected to parts of the first; gives precedence among lodges (Edinburgh first, Kilwinning second, Stirling third), tests of memory ("art of memory") for members, and again stresses written records.

## Stevenson's thesis (summary in our own words)
- Organized, lodge-based freemasonry with its own rituals and secrets took shape in **Scotland around 1600**, not in England around 1700; Schaw's reforms are the decisive moment.
- Scottish lodges kept **minutes** from 1599 onward (Lodge of Edinburgh (Mary's Chapel) and Aitchison's Haven are usually cited as holding the oldest surviving lodge minutes; exact "oldest" claims vary between sources).
- By 1710 some twenty-five such lodges met, or had met, in Scotland; non-operative "gentlemen" members appear in them during the 17th century.
- He links the Scottish lodge culture to Renaissance currents (art of memory, Hermeticism), i.e. a Renaissance rather than Enlightenment origin.
- Debate: this contrasts with historians such as Margaret C. Jacob, who emphasize the 18th-century, Enlightenment and London-centered character of speculative masonry. Stevenson criticized Jacob's later book (*The Origins of Freemasonry*, 2006) for projecting 18th-century freemasonry back into 17th-century Scotland.

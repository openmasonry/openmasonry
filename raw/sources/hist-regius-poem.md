# Clip: The Regius Poem (Halliwell Manuscript), c. 1390–1450

- **What it is:** the oldest known of the *Old Charges* of English stonemasons; the only one in verse (Middle English rhyming couplets).
- **Manuscript:** British Library (ex-Royal Library), Royal MS 17 A I; small quarto on vellum, 64 pages. Cataloged by David Casley (1734) as "A Poem of Moral Duties", title *Constituciones Artis Gemetriae secundum Euclydem*. Given to the British Museum by George II in 1757.
- **Clipped from:**
  - Grand Lodge of British Columbia and Yukon, text page: https://freemasonry.bcy.ca/texts/regius.html (accessed November 2026). Text from H. J. Whymper's 1889 edition (*Constituciones Artis Geometriae Secundum Euclydem*, Spencer & Co., London), correcting Halliwell's 1840 transcription; modern rendering by Roderick H. Baxter (1923). Public domain.
  - Wikipedia, "Regius Manuscript" (orientation only): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regius_Manuscript (accessed November 2026).
- **Author of the poem:** unknown (probably a cleric). Editor: James Orchard Halliwell (paper to the Society of Antiquaries 1838–39, published 1840).

## Dating (disputed)
- Halliwell (1840) and later Woodford and Hughan: c. 1390 or earlier.
- Edward Augustus Bond (British Museum keeper of manuscripts) argued ~50 years later; per Wikipedia's summary, modern palaeographic analysis supports the second quarter of the 15th century (c. 1425–1450).
- So "c. 1390" is the traditional date, not a settled one.

## Content (summary)
- Legendary history: Euclid "counterfeits" geometry and calls it masonry, to teach the children of the nobility in Egypt; the craft comes to England under King Athelstan (r. 924–939). **Legend, not history.**
- Athelstan's assembly sets **fifteen articles** (for master masons) and **fifteen points** (for craftsmen): fair pay, honest work, no stealing of another's work, care of apprentices, annual assemblies.
- The Four Crowned Martyrs (Quatuor Coronati) legend; the seven liberal arts (grammar, dialectic, rhetoric, music, astronomy, arithmetic, geometry, with geometry as the art that separates falsehood from truth); moral aphorisms and table manners; closing blessing.

## Excerpts (public domain, Whymper 1889 text)
Opening lines 1–6:
> Whose wol bothe wel rede and loke,
> He may fynde wryte yn olde boke
> Of grete lordys and eke ladyysse,
> That hade mony chyldryn y-fere, y-wisse;
> And hade no rentys to fynde hem wyth,
> Nowther yn towne, ny felde, ny fryth:

The first article:
> The furste artycul of thys gemetry:--
> The mayster mason moste be ful securly
> Bothe stedefast, trusty, and trwe,
> Hyt schal hum never thenne arewe:
> And pay thy felows after the coste,
> As vytaylys goth thenne, wel thou woste;

(Modern sense: the master mason must be steadfast, trusty and true, and pay his fellows fairly according to the cost of living.)

The fifteen articles and points:
> Fyftene artyculus þey þer sowȝton, and fyftene poyntys þer þey wroȝton.

## Note
The poem is an operative (working stonemasons') regulation wrapped in legend. It contains no modern speculative ritual.

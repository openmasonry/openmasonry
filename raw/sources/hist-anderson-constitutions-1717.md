## The book (facts)
- Commonly called "Anderson's Constitutions" after its main author, the Rev. James Anderson (Scottish Presbyterian minister in London). Approved by Grand Lodge and published 1723; dedication signed by J. T. Desaguliers as Deputy Grand Master.
- Contents: a long **legendary history** of masonry from Adam onwards; **six Charges** (adapted from the Old Charges); the **39 General Regulations** (compiled by George Payne, Grand Master 1720–21); songs.
- Museum of Freemasonry: it helped establish the London Grand Lodge as the governing body of English freemasonry and carried modern freemasonry to the world; the Charges became its moral framework. Second edition 1738 with a much longer (and more embellished) history; French translation by Louis-François de la Tierce, 1742. Benjamin Franklin reprinted the 1723 edition in Philadelphia in 1734.
- Authorship is debated: some argue Desaguliers and Payne shaped the Charges and Regulations more than Anderson.

## Excerpts (public domain, 1723)
Charge I, "Concerning God and Religion":
> A Mason is oblig'd by his Tenure, to obey the moral Law; and if he rightly understands the Art, he will never be a stupid Atheist, nor an irreligious Libertine. But though in ancient Times Masons were charg'd in every Country to be of the Religion of that Country or Nation, whatever it was, yet 'tis now thought more expedient only to oblige them to that Religion in which all Men agree, leaving their particular Opinions to themselves; that is, to be good Men and true, or Men of Honour and Honesty, by whatever Denominations or Persuasions they may be distinguish'd; whereby Masonry becomes the Center of Union, and the Means of conciliating true Friendship among Persons that must else have remain'd at a perpetual Distance.

Charge III, "Of Lodges" (admission):
> The Persons admitted Members of a Lodge must be good and true Men, free-born, and of mature and discreet Age, no Bondmen, no Women, no immoral or scandalous Men, but of good Report.

Charge IV (merit):
> All Preferment among Masons is grounded upon real Worth and personal Merit only ... Therefore no Master or Warden is chosen by Seniority, but for his Merit.

General Regulation VI (ballot):
> But no Man can be enter'd a Brother in any particular Lodge, or admitted to be a Member thereof, without the unanimous Consent of all the Members of that Lodge then present when the Candidate is propos'd ...

General Regulation XXXIX (amendment and landmarks):
> Every Annual Grand-Lodge has an inherent Power and Authority to make new Regulations, or to alter these, for the real Benefit of this ancient Fraternity: Provided always that the old Land-Marks be carefully preserv'd, and that such Alterations and new Regulations be propos'd and agreed to at the third Quarterly Communication preceding the Annual Grand Feast; and that they be offered also to the Perusal of all the Brethren before Dinner, in writing, even of the youngest Apprentice; the Approbation and Consent of the Majority of all the Brethren present being absolutely necessary to make the same binding and obligatory ...

The same regulation records that these Regulations were proposed by Grand Lodge "to about 150 Brethren, on St. John Baptist's Day, 1721".

## The founding narrative
- **Traditional account (UGLE):** on St John's Day, June 24, 1717, four London lodges met at the Goose and Gridiron tavern (St Paul's Churchyard), declared themselves a Grand Lodge and elected Anthony Sayer Grand Master; "the first Grand Lodge in the world". UGLE celebrated its tercentenary in 2017 and the Constitutions' in 2023.
- **Source problem:** the only narrative of the 1716–1717 meetings is Anderson's in the **1738** edition, written two decades later. The 1723 edition does not narrate it.
- **Prescott & Sommers (2017):** argue there is no contemporary evidence for 1717; the Apple Tree tavern named by Anderson apparently did not exist in 1716–17; organized Grand Lodge effectively begins with the Stationers' Hall feast of **June 24, 1721**, when the Duke of Montagu was installed as the first noble Grand Master. They suggest the earlier story served the interests of early figures (e.g. Sayer).
- **Reception:** John Hamill (former UGLE librarian) agrees the evidence for 1717 is lacking; Ric Berman and David Harrison remain unconvinced by 1721 and reject the idea of a deliberate fabrication.
- **Later events (uncontested in outline):** Grand Masters Sayer (1717), George Payne (1718, 1720), Desaguliers (1719), Montagu (1721); rival "Antients" Grand Lodge 1751 (Laurence Dermott, *Ahiman Rezon* 1756); union as the United Grand Lodge of England on December 27, 1813 under the Duke of Sussex. UGLE says the rivals coexisted "neither recognizing each other as regular".

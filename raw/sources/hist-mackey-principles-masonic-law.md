# Clip: Albert G. Mackey, *The Principles of Masonic Law* (1856)

- **URL:** Project Gutenberg eBook #12186: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/12186 (plain text https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/12186/pg12186.txt), accessed November 2026. Public domain.
- **Author:** Albert Gallatin Mackey (1807–1881), physician and Masonic writer of Charleston, South Carolina; Secretary General of the Supreme Council 33° (Southern Jurisdiction).
- **Related:** Mackey's list of **25 Landmarks** first appeared as "The Foundations of Masonic Law", *American Quarterly Review of Freemasonry*, October 1858 (vol. 2, p. 230), later reprinted in his *Text Book of Masonic Jurisprudence* (1859). Grand Lodge of British Columbia and Yukon, "Landmarks history": https://freemasonry.bcy.ca/grandlodge/landmarks_history.html (accessed November 2026).

## Mackey's definition of the Landmarks (verbatim, 1856)
> I am disposed to adopt a middle course, and to define the Landmarks of Masonry to be, all those usages and customs of the craft--whether ritual or legislative--whether they relate to forms and ceremonies, or to the organization of the society--which have existed from time immemorial, and the alteration or abolition of which would materially affect the distinctive character of the institution or destroy its identity.

He adds that laws enacted by Grand Lodges, "no matter how old they may be, do not constitute landmarks, and may, at any time, be altered or expunged", citing Regulation XXXIX of 1723.

## On the ballot (verbatim excerpts)
> The sixth General Regulation requires unanimity in the ballot. ... This regulation, it will be remembered, was adopted in 1721. But in the "New Regulations," adopted in 1754 ... it is said: "but it was found inconvenient to insist upon unanimity in several cases; and, therefore, the Grand Masters have allowed the lodges to admit a member, if not above three black balls are against him; though some lodges desire no such allowance."

> The Grand Lodge of England still acts under this new regulation ... But nearly all the Grand Lodges of this country have adhered to the old regulation ... and by special enactment have made the unanimous consent of all the Brethren present necessary to the election of a candidate.

> The great object of the ballot is, to secure the independence of the voter; and, for this purpose, its secrecy should be inviolate. ... No Mason can be called to an account for the vote which he has deposited.

(Mackey adds that voting against a candidate out of "private pique or personal prejudice" is wrong, but the lodge has no right to judge a member's motives: the classic structure of the unaccountable black ball.)

## On qualifications
- Mackey (verbatim): "No woman can be made a Mason. This landmark is so indisputable, that it would be wholly superfluous to adduce any arguments or authority in its support." He also discusses the requirements of being free-born, of mature age and physically "perfect", positions typical of mid-19th-century American Grand Lodges.
- The "one-fifth of the votes" rejection rule attributed to historical French practice in the OpenMasonry whitepaper (§2) was **not found** in this text; it remains unverified here.

## Landmarks: reception (from the BC&Y page)
- The word "landmarks" appears in Regulation XXXIX (1723) but was never defined there.
- Mackey's 25 (1858) were adopted by a number of North American Grand Lodges; other lists vary widely (e.g. H. B. Grant of Kentucky listed 54 in 1889; Massachusetts adopted a short list in 1918).
- Albert Pike wrote (1888) that the question of what the landmarks are "has never been definitely settled". The Grand Lodge of BC&Y has never adopted any enumeration.

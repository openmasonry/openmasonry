# 1°: Apprentice of the Open Craft (study guide)

**House:** The Craft Lodge. Self-knowledge and the tools of the work.  
**Virtue:** Humility  
**Art:** Grammar and Rhetoric: learning to read the work and speak clearly about it  
**Lesson:** "Before you shape the stone, learn to see the stone that you are."  
**Summary page:** [docs/degrees/degree-01.md](../degrees/degree-01.md) (generated from [`canon/degrees.toml`](../../canon/degrees.toml))

This is the full public study guide of the 1st degree. Everything an Apprentice is asked for is written here or in the documents it links to. How the guides work is explained in the [README](README.md).

## 1. Purpose of the degree

The 1st degree is the door. It makes a person a member of OpenMasonry and gives them a first task: to look honestly at themselves and to learn to read and write about the work clearly.

Everything that follows rests on two abilities: self-knowledge, meaning one's motives, limits and habits known well enough to work with others without deceiving them or oneself; and the ability to read a text closely and say clearly what one thinks of it. A member who cannot read a rule cannot keep it; a member who cannot state an objection clearly cannot defend anyone. The 1st degree trains both, at the smallest possible scale: one person, one declaration, one short reflection.

The Apprentice is not yet asked to build anything for others. The rough stone in the arms of the degree (the charge `rough-ashlar` in the canon) stands for the member as they arrive: material with real worth and real flaws, not yet shaped.

## 2. The virtue: Humility

**What it is.** Humility is an accurate estimate of oneself. It is not low self-esteem and not modesty for show. It is the habit of seeing one's own abilities, mistakes and interests as they are, and of letting that view be corrected by evidence and by other people.

**How it shows in practice.**

- Asking questions before giving opinions, and admitting ignorance when that is the case.
- Reading a rule or a text in full before criticizing it.
- Separating what one has observed from what one infers or hopes.
- Accepting correction without treating it as an attack, and correcting others without humiliating them.
- Acknowledging help: naming the people and the sources one learned from.
- Judging acts, not persons, and oneself first, as the [Declaration](../../canon/eternal.toml) puts it.

**Common misreadings.**

- *Humility as silence.* An Apprentice speaks in the Lodge from the first day; the degree unlocks it. Humility shapes how one speaks (with evidence, questions and honesty about limits), not whether one speaks.
- *Humility as deference to rank.* Degrees grant duties, never weight (Landmark IV). A Master Builder's argument is worth exactly what its reasons are worth. Deferring to a degree instead of a reason is not humility; it is the beginning of a hidden hierarchy.
- *Humility as self-contempt.* Seeing the stone that you are includes seeing what is good in it. A reflection that only lists faults is no more accurate than one that only lists merits.
- *Humility as a performance.* Phrases of modesty that change nothing are vanity in another form. The test is whether one actually changes one's mind when the evidence changes.

## 3. The art: Grammar and Rhetoric

The Craft degrees revive the seven liberal arts as method rather than ornament ([Whitepaper §11.1](../whitepaper/WHITEPAPER.md#111-the-liberal-arts-as-the-core)). The 1st degree takes the first two. In the classical scheme, grammar is the art of reading and writing correctly, and rhetoric the art of speaking and writing persuasively and fittingly. Here they are taught as practical tools for a member of a self-governing body whose rules are written down.

### 3.1 Grammar: reading the work

Grammar begins with close reading. A rule of OpenMasonry, such as an article of the [Constitution](../../CONSTITUTION.md), is a sentence with a subject, a verb, conditions and exceptions. To read it well:

- **Find the actor and the act.** Who may do what? "Only the candidate may open their own examination" has one actor (the candidate) and one act (opening), and the word "only" excludes everyone else.
- **Find the conditions.** Words such as "after", "until", "when", "unless" and "where" carry most of the meaning of a rule. Underline them.
- **Distinguish obligation, permission and prohibition.** "Shall", "may" and "may not" are different instructions. A rule that says a member *may* do something does not oblige them to.
- **Define the terms.** When a word has a precise meaning in the project ("active", "dormant", "lapsed"), find where it is defined before assuming its everyday sense.
- **Paraphrase.** Restate the sentence in your own words and check the paraphrase against the original. If the paraphrase loses a condition, the reading was incomplete.

Grammar also governs writing. A clear sentence has one main idea, a subject close to its verb, and concrete words. Prefer the active voice when the actor matters ("the examiner reports"), because the passive can hide who is responsible ("a report is made").

### 3.2 Rhetoric: speaking clearly about it

Rhetoric is often confused with manipulation. The classical tradition distinguishes them. Aristotle described three modes of persuasion: the character of the speaker (*ethos*), the emotions of the audience (*pathos*) and the argument itself (*logos*). In a fraternity whose decisions are public and whose members judge acts, not persons, the weight falls on *logos*, supported by an *ethos* earned through honesty, and by *pathos* used only to make a real concern felt, never to replace a reason.

The classical canon also divides the work of composition into five parts, which remain a useful checklist for any text:

1. **Invention:** finding what to say. What is the question? What do you actually know?
2. **Arrangement:** putting it in order. A common order is: the point, the reasons, the evidence, the objections, the conclusion.
3. **Style:** choosing the words. Plain, precise and appropriate to the reader.
4. **Memory:** in writing, this becomes knowing the material well enough to answer questions about it.
5. **Delivery:** in writing, the presentation: headings, paragraphs, links and citations that let a reader follow and check.

Good rhetoric in this sense is also fair: it states the strongest version of a view before disagreeing with it, and it admits the limits of its own argument.

### 3.3 Exercises

Do these alone or share them as drafts in the room of the 1st degree. None of them is graded.

1. **Parse a Landmark.** Take Landmark II from [`canon/eternal.toml`](../../canon/eternal.toml). List every actor, every permission and every prohibition it contains. Then write a one-paragraph paraphrase and compare it with the original.
2. **Find the conditions.** In Constitution [Art. III](../../CONSTITUTION.md#article-iii-membership), mark every word that states a condition or a time limit. Write the five steps of admission as a numbered list in your own words.
3. **Rewrite for clarity.** Take a paragraph you wrote some time ago (an email, a note, a post). Rewrite it so that each sentence has a clear actor and one main idea. Compare the lengths.
4. **Steelman.** Choose a rule of OpenMasonry you disagree with. Write the best argument *for* it that you can, in 150 words, before writing your objection.

## 4. The lesson, unpacked

> "Before you shape the stone, learn to see the stone that you are."

The lesson has three parts.

**"Before."** The order matters. A member who sets out to reform others, or the fraternity, before examining themselves will project their own flaws onto what they reform. The first degree comes first for that reason.

**"Shape the stone."** The builder's allegory runs through all the degrees: the work is to turn rough material into something that fits a shared structure. Here the material is the member's own character and abilities. Shaping is not erasing: a stone keeps its nature and gains a form that lets it bear weight beside others.

**"See the stone that you are."** Seeing is an act of attention, and it can be trained. It needs honesty (not hiding what one sees), patience (not deciding too early what one is) and other people (who see what one cannot). Grammar and rhetoric serve this: they are tools for reading one's own words as carefully as a stranger's, and for saying what one finds without exaggeration in either direction.

The lesson also explains the masterwork. The first public reflection is the first written act of seeing.

## 5. The masterwork

The canon states it exactly as follows:

> Signed declaration of the Landmarks (the petition); after admission, a first public reflection (500+ words) at a public URL the member controls, recorded as a work entry. No examination: the admission ballot confers the degree

It has two parts.

### 5.1 The signed Declaration

The [Declaration](../../canon/eternal.toml) is the short text every member accepts, ending with the acceptance of the Landmarks and the freedom to leave at any time. It is signed as part of admission: the invited person's **petition** is an entry signed with their own key that commits to the SHA-256 of the Declaration as written in `canon/eternal.toml` (Constitution [Art. III.2](../../CONSTITUTION.md#article-iii-membership); `bin/om join`). The verifier checks this part: a petition that commits to any other text is refused.

Before signing, read the Declaration and the seven Landmarks in full, using the grammar of section 3.1. Signing is a promise. A person who cannot accept a Landmark should not sign; the Landmarks cannot be amended (Constitution [Art. VII.4](../../CONSTITUTION.md#article-vii-amendments)).

### 5.2 The first public reflection

**What it is.** A text of at least 500 words, written by the member, in which they look at themselves in the light of the degree: why they came, what they bring, what they expect to find hard, and how they read the Declaration and the Landmarks. It is a reflection, not a biography: it should contain no personal data you would not want public for good, and no personal data of anyone else.

**A suggested structure** (about 500 to 1,200 words):

1. **Motive.** Why you come: the honest reason, including any reason you are not proud of.
2. **The stone you are.** Two or three abilities you bring and two or three limits you know of, each with a concrete example.
3. **Reading the Landmarks.** One Landmark you find easy to accept and one you find demanding, with a close reading of its words.
4. **Expectations.** What you want from the Craft degrees, and what you are willing to give.
5. **Sources.** Anything you quoted or drew on.

**Writing it.** Write a first draft quickly, then revise using the five parts of rhetoric (section 3.2). Read it aloud. Cut every sentence that says nothing a reader could check or feel. An Apprentice can share drafts in the room of the 1st degree, ask a sponsor for comments, or share the draft in public. The decision of what to publish stays the author's.

**Citing sources.** Quote briefly and name the author and the work for every quotation and borrowed idea. Any consistent citation style serves; what matters is that a reader can find the source.

**Where it is published, and when.** The reflection is due after admission: under Landmark I the only conditions of admission the fraternity sets are adulthood, sponsorship, the signed Declaration, and the ballot, so the reflection is the Apprentice's first work as a member, not a condition of becoming one. Publish it at a stable public `http(s)` address you control (a page, a repository or a post of your own), and record it in the ledger with `bin/om work <url>`, a signed `work` entry that references it ([SPEC §6.14](../whitepaper/SPEC.md#614-work)). Personal content lives outside the ledger, which keeps only the reference, so that an author can erase their own content ([Whitepaper §5.4](../whitepaper/WHITEPAPER.md#54-privacy) and [§12.3](../whitepaper/WHITEPAPER.md#123-data-protection)). The verifier checks the `work` entry, not the text it points to.

### 5.3 How the 1st degree is conferred

The 1st degree has no examination: the canon sets **0 days** of waiting and **0 examiners**, and says so. It is conferred by the admission ballot, in five steps (Constitution [Art. III.2](../../CONSTITUTION.md#article-iii-membership); [Helping a person take part](../agents/START.md#01-how-admission-works)):

1. **Application.** The person introduces themselves in a public issue, in their own words. It asks for no personal data and gives no right.
2. **Invitation.** A Master Builder who is ready to answer for the person's identity, adulthood and good faith signs an invitation, and becomes the first sponsor. It lapses after 30 days without a petition, and still counts against the sponsor's budget of four sponsorships at a time for 90 days after it lapsed.
3. **Petition.** The invited person signs the petition, committing to the Declaration (section 5.1). It lapses if not admitted within 180 days.
4. **Second sponsor.** A second Master Builder sponsors the petition, where the Constitution requires two ([Art. IV.1](../../CONSTITUTION.md#article-iv-sponsorship)): two once two or more active Master Builders exist. In the Founding Phase (fewer than seven active Master Builders), the inviter alone is enough once the petition has been live 30 days ([SPEC §7.5](../whitepaper/SPEC.md#75-sponsors-and-the-sponsor-budget)).
5. **Ballot.** A sponsor opens the admission ballot. The electors are the members of the 2nd degree and above (the 1st degree only when nobody is present in the 2nd). It runs 7 days to commit and, after a gap of 6 hours 15 minutes, 3 to reveal, and closes early once every elector has revealed. Dormant electors keep their vote; the quorum counts the present ones. The ballot needs a quorum, and the candidate is refused when black balls reach one fifth of the votes cast ([SPEC §7.1 and §7.2](../whitepaper/SPEC.md#72-decide)). A ballot that misses its quorum lapses, with no penalty: the petition stays live, and a sponsor opens another ballot or re-runs the lapsed one once.

Sponsors answer for identity, adulthood and good faith, never for opinions (Constitution [Art. IV.3](../../CONSTITUTION.md#article-iv-sponsorship)).

## 6. Criteria for the work

No examiners are drawn for the 1st degree, so no examination verdict is given, and the reflection comes after the admission ballot, so it is no part of that vote. The criteria below are what a Fellow reviewing Apprentice work will look for (the 2nd degree unlocks that duty), and above all the candidate's own checklist. They follow the general criteria of the [README](README.md#what-an-examiner-looks-for-in-general).

| Criterion | Met when |
|---|---|
| Requirement | The Declaration is signed through the petition, and the reflection has at least 500 words of the author's own text (quotations not counted), is published at a public address the author controls and is recorded as a `work` entry. |
| Virtue (humility) | The reflection names at least one real limit and one real ability with a concrete example, and distinguishes what the author knows from what they hope. |
| Art (grammar and rhetoric) | The text has a discernible structure; at least one Landmark or the Declaration is read closely and paraphrased accurately; sentences can be followed by a reader who does not know the author. |
| Lesson | The text turns attention on the author before it turns it on others or on the fraternity. |
| Authorship and sources | The text is the author's own; quotations and borrowed ideas are attributed. |
| Privacy | The text contains no personal data of anyone else, and none of the author's that they would regret making permanent. |

Not criteria: the author's beliefs or unbelief, their politics, their command of English beyond being understood, their writing style, their profession, or how long they have known any member. Landmark I forbids ranking anyone for origin, belief or class; a reflection is not a test of eloquence.

## 7. Reading

Public-domain classics are cited by title and author; any public-domain translation serves. Open resources are named as published.

- **Plato**, *Apology* and *Phaedrus* (Jowett translation, public domain, on Project Gutenberg). Socratic humility, and a dialogue on what good and bad speech are.
- **Aristotle**, *Rhetoric*, Book I (public-domain translations). The three modes of persuasion.
- **Quintilian**, *Institutio Oratoria* (*The Orator's Education*), Book I (public-domain translations). Grammar as the foundation of the other arts.
- **Confucius**, *Analects*, Book XIII.3 on the "rectification of names" (James Legge translation, public domain). Why exact words matter for order and justice.
- **Marcus Aurelius**, *Meditations* (public-domain translations). Private self-examination as daily practice.
- **Michel de Montaigne**, *Essays*, especially "Of Experience" (Charles Cotton translation, public domain). The essay as a form of self-knowledge.
- **William Strunk Jr.**, *The Elements of Style* (1918 edition, public domain, on Project Gutenberg). Short rules for plain English.
- **OpenStax**, *Writing Guide with Handbook* (open textbook). Drafting, revising and citing sources.
- The project's own texts: the [Landmarks and the Declaration](../../canon/eternal.toml), the [Constitution](../../CONSTITUTION.md) and the [rules of the rooms](../governance/ROOMS.md).

## 8. Questions for the room

These prompts are for conversation in the room of the 1st degree. Remember the rules of the room: nothing said there decides anything, no one is discussed who cannot read it, and no ballot or candidate is canvassed there.

1. Which word of the Declaration did you find hardest to sign, and why?
2. What is the difference between humility and silence? When is staying silent a failure of humility?
3. Landmark IV says degrees grant duties, never weight. How would you notice, in a conversation, that someone's degree was weighing more than their reasons?
4. Take one sentence of the Constitution that seemed clear at first and turned out to hide a condition. What did a close reading change?
5. What does the Declaration's promise to judge acts, not persons, and oneself first ask of you in a disagreement?
6. How can a text about oneself be honest without exposing what should stay private?

## 9. What the degree unlocks

The canon states: "Speak in the Lodge, open issues, join study circles". An Apprentice also reads the room of the 1st degree, as every member does ([rules of the rooms §2](../governance/ROOMS.md#2-who-reads-which-room)).

These are duties and eligibility, never weight (Landmark IV). Every member holds exactly one vote in each ballot they are eligible for. In ordinary times an Apprentice is an elector only of an admission ballot in which nobody is present in the 2nd degree; voting in Lodge ballots is unlocked by the 2nd degree.

## 10. Toward the 2nd degree

After 30 days in the 1st degree, an Apprentice may open the examination for the 2nd degree with a published masterwork. The [guide of the 2nd degree](degree-02.md) describes it. The waiting period is a minimum, not a deadline: there is time to study.
